Your Retention Rate Dropped. Or Did It?

Why a single year can’t answer that question – and what three simulations show about how long it actually takes to know your retention rates are changing.

By Steven Sherrin, Ph.D. (Wentworth Institute of Technology)

Retention rates are make-or-break numbers for most schools. A two-point gain is a great year; a two-point loss can mean a hiring freeze or budget cuts. So it’s understandable that leaders want an answer to the question “Are we improving our retention rates?” – and want it quickly.

The trouble is that a single year’s number often can’t give them one.

What I hope to convince you of is that retention rates often do not accurately reflect your institution’s time, effort, and resources each year. A lot of what changes your most recent retention rate may have nothing to do with that – but rather, with a random roll of the dice.

Randomness Is More Important than You Think

A college with the same “true” retention rate – its actual ability to retain students – will still see a different retention rate from year to year due to random chance. While many of us know this on a rational level, it’s easy to underestimate, intuitively, how much chance alone can move the number.

Below is a quick experiment to see it. Imagine one college whose true retention rate is fixed at 78 percent and never moves; the only thing that changes from year to year is the luck of the draw. Now simulate that one year over again, 20 times. Try it yourself:

One year, twenty times

The true rate is exactly 78% every single time. Each dot is a reported rate for the identical year.

Now, consider the question: which one of the 20 dots is the “true” retention rate? It isn’t possible to point to it on the chart, because the truth is not any single dot. It is the vertical dotted line: the yearly average over the long run. No institution ever actually gets to observe it. In reality, this year’s retention rate could have been any one of those 20 dots. Yet an institution only gets to report one of them, and that one number becomes the “true” retention rate.

With an entering class of 1,000, 20 “rolls” of the same year typically vary four to six points, with nothing real ever changing. An ordinary two-point swing up or down, the kind that could trigger emergency meetings or task forces, sits comfortably inside that range.

Real Change Is Small

Research shows that genuine improvement or decline in retention usually comes in small amounts, often smaller than a single year of data can reliably detect.

Consider COVID-19, a generational disaster that upended every campus in the country. For most schools, it knocked retention down only a point or two. If a global pandemic is worth two points, the work of a new retention initiative is unlikely to be worth more in a similarly quick timeframe.

In practical terms: if your retention truly did improve or decline in a real but small way (as is common for most schools) there is a good chance you will not see it – at least, not immediately. Instead, your real retention gain can hide inside the noise for years. And the reverse happens just as easily, with an ordinary year of luck presenting itself as progress or loss that was never there.

The simulation below follows a college whose true retention rate really did rise one full percentage point this year, and shows how likely an up or down year is in that situation.

One real improvement, twenty times

This college’s true retention rate really did rise this year – by exactly the amount you set, from a starting point of 78%. The simulation rolls that same one-year change 20 times; each dot is the change the college would actually have reported.

In this case, ask yourself: how does a school in this scenario know if it is truly improving? Run it a few times and the pattern is clear: real changes do not always produce positive results. In this particular case, a school with 1,000 entering students that truly improved retention by one percentage point, a small but meaningful feat, did not always see that improvement; in fact, posting 6 down years out of 20 scenarios.

Small Changes Take Years to Trust

So if one year can’t be trusted, how many years do you need instead? We can test that through simulations, as well. The bottom line is that real changes can take several years, or more, to truly be seen.

The simulation below starts with an institution whose initiative worked: its “true” retention rate improved by anywhere from 1 to 5 percentage points (you can set how much in the simulator). However, the institution doesn’t know the “true” impact of the initiative; that would be impossible to know. Therefore, it judges the initiative’s success by what it can see: the actual observed retention rate changes each year.

In the simulation, you can decide how much of an improvement the institution needs as evidence to declare the initiative a success. The question is: how many years of data does it take for a school to reliably see that improvement, and how often does it see too little improvement (or none at all) and conclude that the initiative failed?

Will the numbers catch a gain that really happened?

Every simulated college here truly improved by the amount you choose. The only thing standing between the college and the truth is ordinary luck: which students happen to come back each year.

Take the example shown above: a college with an entering class of 1,000 whose true retention rate rose 2 points. It is hoping for a two-percentage-point increase, but it would settle for an improvement of half the true effect (one percentage point) as strong enough proof of success to keep funding for the initiative.

How long would the school need to wait to reliably see that improvement (9 times out of 10)? The simulation’s answer: 6 years. If the school waits less than 6 years, there’s a significant chance it will prematurely judge the initiative a failure, even though it worked. Even if they wait 6 years, there’s still about a 1 in 10 chance the school won’t see the gain it’s looking for, of at least 1 percentage point.

Communicating to Your Leaders

This article argues that a single year’s retention rate often carries less signal than the weight we tend to place on it. It can move on its own when nothing has changed. It can also fail to move when something real is happening. Real change rarely shows up in a single year, and it can take many years before you can be confident that what you’re seeing is real.

The main thing I wish to say to leaders is that there is a cost to impatience when interpreting retention rates. A genuinely improving school could have a statistically unlucky year – for instance, its retention rate drops a couple points – and its leaders may cut the advising program that was, in fact, working. On the other hand, a declining college could get a lucky statistical draw – “achieving” a two-point gain – and miss negative trends on campus that are not yet visible in the data.

It’s important for institutional researchers to remember that even though a more cautious approach to interpreting retention rates is an improvement (in a purely statistical sense), it is also true that it can make leaders’ decisions more challenging. To leaders, being told that results may take years to show up, and may never be fully certain, could be interpreted as saying that retention can’t be reliably changed or that they can’t succeed on the timeline they’ve promised. There may be others on campus willing to offer simpler stories, that they need to reckon with, too.

What can institutional researchers do to help leaders make the best decisions? A few things can help. First, report multi-year averages of retention rates so that leaders don’t overinterpret year-to-year changes. Second, researchers can set reasonable expectations, ideally before an initiative launches, for how many years it may take to observe a change. Third, when a retention change does appear, researchers can look for evidence beneath it: for example, whether the students an initiative served actually did better than similar students who weren’t served (though, of course, the same uncertainty issues still apply).

So when this year’s retention rate drops and someone asks whether the initiative failed, the most honest answer may be: “It’s too early to tell – and here’s how long it will take to know.”
