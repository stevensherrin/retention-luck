Did Your Retention Rate Actually Improve or Decline This Year? You May Not Be Able to Tell.

Statistics show that much of what moves the number each year is neither effort nor strategy, but a roll of the dice.

By Steven Sherrin, PhD (Wentworth Institute of Technology)

Retention rates are make-or-break numbers for most schools. A two-point gain is a great year; a two-point loss can mean a hiring freeze or budget cuts. It’s understandable that college leaders anxiously await each year’s retention figures and take them seriously.

Yet, there is one fact many retention discussions fail to consider: a lot of what changes your retention rate from one year to the next may have nothing to do with your effort or your strategy. Rather, it can be luck; essentially, a roll of the dice.

Institutions may not be particularly thrilled to welcome this statistical reality; institutional decision-making, budgets, and reputations often hinge on the number. However, what I present below will demonstrate how untrustworthy yearly retention rates can be; and, how real changes in retention are often too small or too slow to reliably appear in a single year.

First: Randomness Is More Important than You Think

A retention rate is measured on a finite group of people, and a finite group never lands on exactly the same number twice, just as 1,000 flips of the same coin will not give you 500 heads every time. Even a college that never changes – same programs, same kind of students, the same true odds that any one of them comes back – will post a different retention rate from year to year.

Topics like random variation and margin of error are likely familiar to many readers who encounter institutional data like these. However, what is less often noticed is just how much it affects year-to-year change.

Below is a quick experiment to see it. Imagine one college whose true retention rate is fixed at, say, 78 percent and never moves; the only thing that changes from year to year is the luck of the draw. Now simulate that one year over again, 20 times. Try it yourself:

One year, twenty times

The true rate is exactly 78% every single time. Each dot is a reported rate for the identical year.

Note that the spread of possible outcomes is often large. With an entering student class of 1,000, 20 rolls of the same year regularly vary four to six points – with nothing “real” ever changing. An ordinary two-point retention rate swing – the kind that could trigger emergency meetings or task forces – sits comfortably inside that range.

Now, let’s ask a related question: what is your true retention rate? Try to point to it on the chart. You can’t, because it is not any single dot. Yet, this is exactly what you are asked to assume when you look at this year’s reported retention rate.

Your institution’s statistical reality is that this year’s retention rate could have been any one of those 20 dots on the chart. The yearly roll of the dice gave you either a lucky or unlucky draw. And that draw can have quite significant implications for your institution.

Second: Real Change Is Often Slow, Small, and Difficult to See

Research shows that genuine improvement (or decline) in retention usually arrives a) slowly, b) over several years, and c) in small amounts – often smaller than a single year of data can reliably detect.

Consider COVID-19, a generational disaster that upended every campus in the country. For most schools it knocked retention down only a point or two. If a global pandemic is worth two points, the work of a new retention initiative is hardly likely to be worth more.

In practical terms: if your retention truly did improve or decline in a real but small way, as is common for most schools, there is a good chance you will not see it in a single year. In actuality, a real retention gain can hide inside the noise for years. And the reverse happens just as easily, with an ordinary year of luck presenting itself as progress or loss that was never there.

A college that improves one percentage point a year—a meaningful feat—still posts losing years anyway. And this doesn’t just happen at small schools, either, but ones with thousands of students (though to a lesser extent).

Taking This to Your Leaders

This article argues that a single year’s retention rate often carries less signal than the weight we tend to place on it. It can move on its own when nothing has changed, and it can fail to move when something real is happening.

The consequence is that a single year can easily point your institution’s leaders the wrong way. A genuinely improving college could have a statistically unlucky year – for instance, its retention rate drops a couple points – and its leaders may cut the advising program that was, in fact, working. On the other hand, a flat or declining college could get a lucky statistical draw – “achieving” a two-point gain – and give credit to a strategy that never actually had a positive impact.

How do we talk to university leaders about this? Before presenting them these findings, it may be important to first appreciate that this may not be especially welcome news.

Leaders are hired to move the numbers, so the suggestion that institutions are not fully in control of the numbers can sound like a threat to their abilities. There may also be other departments who are more willing to offer certainty in their data perspectives, and who provide narratives delivered with total confidence.

Our job may be to persuade leaders that responding to statistical uncertainty with patience and restraint is a sign of strong leadership – not a “failure to act”. A strong leader can hold a steady hand through the ups and downs of a yearly retention rate that doesn’t always reflect how an institution is truly faring.
